Participatory Budgeting Implementation Guide

A practical starting point for involving young people in deciding how public or organizational funds are spent.

Download the Participatory Budgeting guide

Foundations

  • Get funding, scope, and a promise to implement winning projects in writing. Name a government liaison for every topic area.

Design and Recruit

  • Recruit across school types, community groups, and opportunity youth. State the real time commitment. Let youth set the rules and train them with practice, such as mock pitches.

Idea Collection

  • Collect ideas online and in person at community centers and neighborhood forums. Make submitting an idea quick and easy.

Proposal Development

  • Give every committee a liaison, recurring check-ins, a proposal template, and a direct line to staff.

Voting

  • Open the vote to all eligible youth, in simple language and multiple languages, with more than one way to vote. Say where policy priorities go.

Implement and Evaluate

  • Post a status update for every winning project. Hold a meeting where youth present policy priorities to decision-makers. Debrief everyone.

What to avoid

  • Drawn from Augsberger, Gecker, and Collins's case study of Boston's Youth Lead the Change program.

1. Don't front-load support and then fade out.

  • In any youth PB process, the middle stages (turning ideas into feasible, costed proposals) are the hardest and most bureaucratic, so that is where support needs to be strongest, not weakest.

2. Don't make youth depend on a single intermediary for government access.

  • Staff acting as a “middle man” between youth and city officials slowed things down and created inconsistent information. Any program should give youth a direct, reliable channel to the agency or department staff who will actually implement their projects.

3. Don't be vague about time and expectations.

  • Hidden or growing time demands drove attrition. This applies everywhere: state the real workload up front, and plan for school, sports, jobs, and family obligations, with a low-stigma way to step back in after missed meetings.

4. Don't limit the scope without being honest about it.

  • Wherever the scope is limited (by law, by funding source, or by a sponsoring agency), say so clearly at the start and create a defined route for broader priorities to reach decision-makers. Otherwise participants feel their input vanished.

5. Don't recruit youth through one avenue.

  • Programs that recruit through student councils, honors programs, or application processes will skew toward high-achieving, college-bound youth. Recruitment should be intentional and varied (different school types, community organizations, out-of-school youth), and the voting stage should be open to all youth in the jurisdiction, not just those in the core group.

6. Don't assume formal city-style participation fits every young person.

  • Meetings, budgets, and ballots mirror government, which can feel alienating to marginalized youth. Pair formal steps with community-based outreach, accessible venues, and peer-led engagement.

7. Don't risk tokenism.

  • Programs with advisory-only outcomes or no commitment to fund winning projects risk tokenism, which is a core failure of youth participation.

8. Don't skip tracking and evaluation.

  • Collect demographic and participation data at every stage, including who leaves and why, so equity problems are visible. Also plan to follow up on implementation, since youth stay motivated when they see winning projects actually built.

9. Don't leave out voters and idea submitters.

  • Design the process so that idea submitters and voters get something meaningful too, such as clear information about what was funded and why.
